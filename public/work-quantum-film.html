IWhat it is

Film stocks whose crystals are laid by quantum circuits, and a fixer that makes every print of a roll the same, bit for bit, on every machine (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:3).

IIWhat it is not

  • Not a quantum advantage. At these sizes both quantum laws are classically simulable. That is exactly what makes them checkable (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:174)
  • Not deterministic at the shot. A roll from a device is unique (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:178)
  • Not on hardware yet. (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:181)
  • Not a model of any real film's grain. (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:183)

IIIThe contract

“The authority is quantum_film/golden: pure Python and mpmath at 256 bits, with exact counter-based uniforms (SHA-256) (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:196) It imports nothing it could share with what it judges (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:197); a test holds that mechanically (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:198).” The circuits, the float paths and Atlas's results are all scored against it (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:198).

VIIProve it wrong

Cheapest first; the site-wide rules of engagement are stated once, on the Corrections page — not yet built, so this dossier does not link it.

  1. Look at the first prints yourself and compare the structure-factor claim against what the eye can actually see in the published images — free.
  2. make verify-quick on a clean clone, and read whether The negative control, and its twin (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:208) still catches a moved crystal.
  3. Feed the fixer a record with one crystal moved yourself, by hand, and confirm it is refused for its digest and for no other reason.
  4. Repeat the same Atlas job and check whether the bytes really differ: “Identical requests to Atlas returned different bytes wherever sampling was involved (Quantum-Film a7f02db README.md:168),” which needs an Atlas account this dossier does not supply.
