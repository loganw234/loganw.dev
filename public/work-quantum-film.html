IWhat it is

Film stocks whose crystals are laid by quantum circuits, and a fixer that makes every print of a roll the same, bit for bit, on every machine (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:3).

IIWhat it is not

  • Not a quantum advantage. At these sizes both quantum laws are classically simulable. That is exactly what makes them checkable (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:269)
  • Not deterministic at the shot. A roll from a device is unique (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:273)
  • Not on hardware yet. (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:276)
  • Not a model of any real film's grain. (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:278)

IIIThe contract

“The authority is quantum_film/golden: pure Python and mpmath at 256 bits, with exact counter-based uniforms (SHA-256) (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:291) It imports nothing it could share with what it judges (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:292); a test holds that mechanically (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:293).” The circuits, the float paths and Atlas's results are all scored against it (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:293).

VIIProve it wrong

Cheapest first; the site-wide rules of engagement are stated once, on the Corrections page.

  1. Look at the first prints yourself and compare the structure-factor claim against what the eye can actually see in the published images — free.
  2. make verify-quick on a clean clone, and read whether The negative control, and its twin (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:303) still catches a moved crystal.
  3. Feed the fixer a record with one crystal moved yourself, by hand, and confirm it is refused for its digest and for no other reason.
  4. Repeat the same Atlas job and check whether the bytes really differ: “Identical requests to Atlas returned different bytes wherever sampling was involved (Quantum-Film 3a73823 README.md:263),” which needs an Atlas account this dossier does not supply.
